Learn · Tiny Adventure

What it does

Write a little text adventure and play it straight away. Make rooms, join them with exits, hide objects, set a goal, then type go north and see if your world holds together.

Games made of words

Text adventures appeared in the mid-1970s on university computers: there were no pictures, just descriptions, and you typed short commands such as GO NORTH or TAKE LAMP. By the early 1980s they were selling on home computers across the UK, and many people learned to type, read closely and map spaces because of them.

Why they are good for writing

Every room needs a description that makes you want to go somewhere else. Every object needs a reason to exist. Teachers have long used text adventures for reading and retelling, because players have to read carefully to make progress and they love telling each other how they got stuck. Writing one is even better: you have to think about what your reader knows and when.

How this one works

Each room has a name, a description, some exits written as north: hall (one per line), and objects written as key: a small brass key. The goal is to carry one object into one room. Players can type look, go north (or just n), take, drop, examine, inventory and help.

Try this

  1. Play the sample, then add a room called garden off the kitchen.
  2. Write a three-room game set somewhere you know, such as your street or your school.
  3. Hide the goal object somewhere nobody would think to look.
  4. Swap story files with a friend and try to finish each other's game.
