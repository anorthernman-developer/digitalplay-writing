Learn · Cut-Up Machine

What it does

Paste in your own words, cut them into pieces with digital scissors, and shuffle until a line you would never have written turns up. Lock the bits you like and shake the rest.

Words out of a hat

In 1920 the Dada poet Tristan Tzara published instructions for making a poem by cutting a newspaper article into single words, shaking them in a bag and copying them out in the order they came. It was a joke and a serious point at once: who says a poem needs a poet's careful plan?

The cut-up

Around 1959 in Paris, the painter Brion Gysin noticed that slicing through a stack of newspapers with a knife made strange new sentences where the strips met. The writer William S. Burroughs took the method up and used it for years. The fold-in is a cousin: fold one page in half, lay it against another, and read straight across.

Why it works

Your brain cannot help hunting for meaning, so random neighbours spark ideas you would never reach on purpose. The trick is the editing afterwards: the machine gives you raw material, and you choose what stays.

Try this

  1. Paste the last three texts you sent and cut by phrases.
  2. Use Fold-in with a recipe in A and a weather forecast in B.
  3. Lock one fragment you love and shuffle until a whole line works around it.
  4. Cut by words, keep only twelve, and make a title.

Keys

  • S shuffle unlocked fragments
  • On a fragment: L lock, Delete remove, Alt+←/Alt+→ move it
